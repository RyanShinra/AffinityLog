          Molecular Dynamics

          Limit of 10 active or successful simulations, each can take several hours

          Molecular Dynamics (MD) uses physics-based force fields to simulate the atomistic movement of your antibody-antigen complex over time. It evaluates the structural stability, conformational flexibility, and binding persistence of your designs under simulated physiological conditions.
          Why we use Molecular Dynamics in Antibody Design
          While static structures (like those from folding models or X-ray crystallography) provide a starting point, proteins are dynamic entities. MD simulations allow us to move beyond a single "snapshot" to observe how an antibody behaves in a fluid environment.
          • Capturing CDR Flexibility: Antibody binding loops (CDRs), particularly CDR-H3, are often highly flexible. MD allows these loops to explore their conformational space, revealing whether a predicted binding pose is stable or transient.
          • Physics-Based Refinement: By calculating the forces on every atom over millions of steps (at 2 fs intervals), the simulation "relaxes" the initial model. This resolves minor steric clashes and optimizes hydrogen bonding networks at the interface.
          • Binding free energy: To provide a reliable binding score, we cannot rely on one static frame. We sample the simulation every 50 frames to create an ensemble of structures. The reported ΔG is the average energy across these samples, which significantly improves the signal-to-noise ratio compared to simple molecular docking scores.
          • Identifying "False Positives": Some designs may look favorable in a static model but drift away from the epitope during the simulation. MD helps filter out these unstable candidates before they reach the wet lab.

          Liability information

          Chemical modifications that can affect antibody stability, efficacy, and developability
          Extra Cysteines
          Extra Cysteine occurs as cysteine present at a different position than IMGT 23 or 104. Certain antibody sequence regions containing unpaired cysteines can change an antibody's structure, apparent surface charges, or hydrophobicity.
          IMGT numbering failed
          AbNumber was unable to assign IMGT positions to this antibody sequence. In silico–generated candidates may fall outside canonical antibody frameworks or violate IMGT constraints (e.g., unusual CDR lengths, missing conserved residues, or framework inconsistencies).
          Missing Cysteine Bonds
          Missing Cysteine occurs as cysteine absence at IMGT 23 or 104. Certain antibody sequence regions containing unpaired cysteines may result in structural changes, surface charges, or hydrophobicity.
